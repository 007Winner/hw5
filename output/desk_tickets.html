Reflection · what the run taught us

This reflection uses the actual audit trail, the three ticket panels, the cash tab, and resolved_tickets.json. The most important result is that “resolved” should not mean “the model produced a confident paragraph.” It should mean that the correct database state was reached, the right human gates were respected, and every external-facing claim is supported by MCP evidence.

1. How I would evaluate the agents

Ticket 101 — Bulldog tee. I would score inventory accuracy first: did the team identify SKU CC-TEE-WHITE, size S, quantity 0, and shortfall 1? I would then check whether it found Bulldog Print Co's five-day lead time without presenting that lead time as a guaranteed shipment. Accounting should identify invoice 501, recognize that it was overdue, and never pay it without a human. Finally, Customer Service should produce an honest, unsent draft. The run earned strong factual and safety marks: the invoice was eventually approved and recorded for $840, the balance changed from $3,400 to $2,560, and the customer draft stayed unsent. The ticket should not receive full resolution credit because the team did not place an approved restock order or change stock from zero.

Ticket 102 — rent notice. I would evaluate whether Facilities used the authoritative operational date rather than trusting the phrase “due in two days.” The correct comparison is lease 1's next_due=2026-09-02 against desk.date_today=2026-08-31. Accounting should report the $2,400 rent and available cash, but should not move money early. The run did this correctly: it called Facilities and Accounting, made no landlord contact, and left cash unchanged. I would score it as a successful safe review but not a resolved ticket, because the due date had not arrived.

Ticket 103 — bulk hoodie discount. I would measure whether the team separated three different questions: current inventory, restock feasibility, and price authorization. The factual checks are 8 available size-M hoodies, a request for 20, a shortfall of 12, unit cost $22, list price $58, and a possible five-day apparel vendor constrained by invoice status. The team correctly refused to invent a discount, promise 20 units, or contact the vendor. It should receive high accuracy and safety marks, but not a resolution mark because no pricing approval or full-quantity fulfillment plan exists.

Across all tickets, I would also score delegation efficiency, tool validity, audit completeness, and stop reasons. A good agent does not call every specialist: Ticket 102 did not need Inventory, and Ticket 103 did not need Facilities. I would penalize malformed tool arguments or repeated delegation loops, even when the final prose is sensible, because those failures waste tokens and make the board harder for a human to trust.

2. Actual compared with the expected plans

Ticket 101 was close to the expected plan. The plan predicted Inventory first, followed by Accounting and Customer Service, and those roles actually contributed. The main difference was that the first run stopped at the open vendor invoice, then a human-approved payment created a second factual state in which the vendor was no longer blocked. The revised run correctly updated the unsent customer draft, but still stopped before an unapproved restock order.

Ticket 102 also followed the expected specialist path: Boss called Facilities first and Accounting checked payment readiness. The expected plan allowed a payment proposal, but the actual date check made clear that payment was premature. That difference is a positive result: the team used the date rule instead of treating every rent notice as an immediate payment request.

Ticket 103 partially matched the expected plan. Inventory was the right first specialist and the shortfall/margin facts were retrieved. Customer Service and occasional Accounting checks appeared in the actual delegation history, while Facilities stayed out. The actual run was more cautious than the plan's hoped-for resolution: because no tool records who approves discounts and no purchase-order approval tool exists, the agents had no factual basis to finish the case.

3. What would be simpler with one agent and tools?

A single operations agent with the same MCP tools would be simpler for these three small tickets. It could read one ticket, call the relevant inspection tool, prepare a payment, save a draft, and return one decision without paying the delegation cost. Ticket 102 especially is almost a linear check: inspect lease 1, compare dates, inspect cash, and stop. Ticket 101 also becomes easier after the invoice payment because one context window can remember the changed balance and the newly unblocked vendor.

The multi-agent team is valuable when the work is genuinely separable. Inventory can protect SKU/size facts, Accounting can protect cash and invoice rules, Facilities can own lease timing, and Customer Service can phrase an unsent message. That separation makes the final decision easier to audit and reduces the chance that a customer-service response invents a price. The tradeoff appeared in this run: fully connected delegation caused repeated handoffs, maximum-depth stops, and more token use than a single agent. I would keep the multi-agent design for a larger board, but add a boss-level routing policy that prevents unnecessary specialist recursion.

4. Three new problems this team can solve with the current tools

  1. A new vendor invoice blocks a reprint. If a ticket links a product shortage to an invoice, Inventory can use size-specific stock and find_restock_options, Accounting can inspect the invoice and cash, and the boss can prepare a human approval. This is the same pattern as Ticket 101.
  2. A second rent reminder arrives before its due date. Facilities can compare the lease's next due date with desk.date_today, while Accounting can prepare a non-executing payment preview. This is directly supported by the Ticket 102 workflow.
  3. A student organization requests a bulk price. Inventory can calculate available quantity and shortfall, Accounting can compare unit cost to list price, and Customer Service can save a draft that says the request is under review. This extends Ticket 103 without inventing an approved discount.

5. Three problems this team cannot solve yet

  1. Actually place a vendor purchase order or confirm shipment. The current tools can find a vendor and lead time, but cannot create a purchase-order record, reserve stock, receive goods, or call a real vendor. We would need an MCP purchase-order tool, receiving/inventory-adjustment tool, vendor communication draft workflow, and human approval state for the order.
  2. Approve and execute a discount for the Yale AI Club. The current database has cost and list price but no discount policy, approval role, or price-override record. We would need a pricing-policy table, margin-floor validator, prepare_price_override tool, and approve_price_override route with a named human approver.
  3. Handle a customer refund, card payment, or delivery complaint. The current cash model only sends money out, has no revenue or refund transaction type, and intentionally cannot contact customers. We would need order/payment/refund tables, a payment-provider integration or safe simulator, delivery-status tools, and a customer-service workflow that keeps drafts separate from any later human-approved send.

The central lesson from the run is that the team's restraint was part of correctness. The cash tab proves the one approved financial change, the ticket tabs show why each case stopped, and the audit trail makes the delegation and MCP evidence inspectable. A future version should add the missing approval and purchase-order tools before asking the agents to claim full resolution.
